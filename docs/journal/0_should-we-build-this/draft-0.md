# Chatrack blog #0 - Language as a chisel for sound.

## Background

It's February 2022, I am the happiest man in the world because I just relased my first real song through a label, "Spin Cycle". A house track that I produced.

This was the culmination of a 10 year journey from the age of 14, the first time I opened GarageBand on our family Mac and started playing around. Which was the end of a prior 10 year journey of playing the piano.

Nothing can replace the journey of those 10 years. From being frustrated that I couldn't make anything sound good, to seeking out education and waking up in the mornings at 5am to produce before work. Each track got slightly better, I made about 100 full tracks. Going to a Master's in audio in part because plugins fascinated me as a unique software problem where the result is a tool for making art that can also be considered a type of art in itself, the same way architecture or design could be. But also for the experience of learning deeply the foundational concepts in shaping sound. And working on school projects in cool studios didn't hurt :) There were some long nights, lots of frustration, but also, a few moments where I thought "hey, this is actually sounding not half bad". 

I started studying AI in 2019 as part of my computer science degree. It was your classical Bayesian foundations - probability, search algorithms, getting a robot to navigate a maze by applying an algorithm and translating it into choice of actions, that sort of thing. Towards the end of college I felt like I had read about a lot of interesting projects at the interesection of AI and audio and I wanted to explore that overlap as part of my Master's degree work. I did just that, working with a professor on getting a neural network to model an analog compressor (the LA2A) and generating syntehtic traning data with digital effects to see if the same model could learn distortion, compression, and reverb (distortion was good, compressor ok, reverb was awful)

I've always been fascinated by how deeply technical music production and mixing actually are. Musical creativity is only half the battle. The real lift is knowing how to tehnically translate your idea into the real world using the tools at your disposal.

When Cursor rolled out over the past two years I started to try it to work on software engineering work. At the time, ironically, the main project I was working on was a plugin of my own. It was a bigger scale than any software project I had attempted and I was going it alone. When I got stuck I started copying and pasting code into ChatGPT, this is when Cursor was still pretty much a fork of VSCode with tab autocomplete -- nowhere near the chat-first abstraction over the actual code that it is today. I remember the exact problem I was stuck on - the graphics drawing code had a bug where the input and output waveforms were slightly out of sync in the animation, creating this "lagging" effect. I remember being utterly discouraged and stuck on this, crawling the Juce forums, learning about timers, and then vSync, and then OpenGL. This is the point where I decided I would learn OpenGL because I was fascinated by it but also the point where I started to lean heavily on ChatGPT to get me up and running. In the end, it worked, and the shaders are written largely by the model. But I also feel like I understand the concepts behind it. That last part is a tension which I find myself navigating increasingly as the coding models get better and better - the temptation to hand off the solution to the model without understanding what's happening. We may not really know the impact of that until a few years down the line and we're having to fix bugs but we don't understand the original code in the first place...

Point is, nowadays, I don't write much code by hand at all. My thoughts on this could be a whole separate essay. But to me, in that time, the gain of expanding the possibility of the results I could accomplish was worth the tradeoff of losing the highs and lows of struggling with a problem to figure it out. The struggle transferred to product taste and communication of details, rather than translation of desired functionality into a coding language. To be honest, I was blown away by the technology like I have never been in my nearly 28 years on earth. In the context of my project, it gave me the confidence that I could complete the challenge of creating a great and beautiful product within an acceptable timeline (ended up being 2 years, the product is [ClipperX](https://www.benderaudio.com/products/clipperx?srsltid=AU7gw4VCyc6DELZpAQMU5BFBCywglA80X48MkCP_Is2cvhho0Kj2besG)).

Fast forward to 2026 and companies like Suno and Mozart and others have started to productize the notion of having a generative audio model that you can talk to. Describe your idea and watch it come out the other side of the black box. Ethics of data sourcing aside, I never could quite get behind this paradigm. From a creativity standpoint, to me it defeated the whole point. To me it seems like you remove too much of the control over what artistically gets said. But I am biased, because of the 10 year journey I described before. If I had Suno when I was 14, I can't say whether I would have gone down the same path of trying to master music production. Maybe it would have been "close enough" to just have the model do it. Maybe the engineer in me would not have been satisfied and would have dug deeper into the "how" anyway. That isn't what we're studying here, but it helps frame a fundamental assumption of this project: Humans in the loop. Keep the creative decision making in the hands of the artist, at a more granular level than just "getting pretty close", while still taking advantage of the abstraction over technical operation and the problem of knowing what you want to do, but not knowing the knobs to turn to do it. 

## Two modes of creativity (my own mental model)



#### Creativity as engineering

"I know exactly what I want to make in my head it's fully realized. I can hear the fully produced song clearly. The challenge is how to produce the exact result that I currently only hold in my imagination".

#### Creativity as play

"I have no idea what I want to make, but I want to make *something*. I'll start with a blank sheet of paper or a blank DAW session and start tweaking knobs. I have no idea what the final product will be but I'll get started and it will come to me over time. The challenge is generating possible choices and then picking one direction to keep based on my taste.

I have always been driven by the first one. I almost never find the energy to sit in the studio tweaking knobs, unless I have a clear vision of what I want to make. This provides the activation energy to sit down and make it (historically, skipping class or work because the impulse to bring the thing to life is overwhelmingly strong - over time, I have learned to say no to this when the consequences would upset my stability long term, like not working enough hours as a freelancer because my day turns into unpaid studio time).

Chatrack is inherently suited to help those who are of the first type. It does not facilitate play. For that you are probably reaching for your generative models, or some kind of generative algorithm. What Chatrack can do is reduce the technical friction on the way to making the thing which you already know exactly what it is. The advantage of this is that it can keep you from getting stuck in rabbit holes. We have all been there as producers starting out - you have a great idea but you don't know how to work a synth to produce the exact sound that you want. As a result you spend the next hour playing with knobs on the synth trying to get close, or even figuring out how to work the software in the first place. When this happens, it can kill creative flow. Your brain no longer has room to store the original idea and needs to switch from creative mode to analytical mode. With every minute that you spend in analytical mode, the resolution of the creative idea starts to decay rapidly. It's not like you can save it in your brain and come back to it. That part of the brain just doesn't work like that. Neuroscience people may be able to elaborate more here but that's as far as I can get it and will leave it there. In "Flow" theory, this is what kills the flow state. Things no longer hit the sweet spot between technical challenge and creative impetus. The balance is thrown off. As a result, momentum can be lost, you're "not feeling it anymore" and maybe you start thinking of all the other important things you should be doing. You might save the project with the intention of coming back to it, but now the activation energy required to pick up where you left off is massive. Plus, you don't have the initial excitement of the idea being fresh anymore. This was my experience for a long time. 

## Why build Chatrack (isn't using AI "cheating"?)

The fundamental hypothesis that Chatrack explores is whether adding an abstraction layer on top of the technical process of production / mixing that is less cognitively demanding (natural language, which in my understanding lives in a different part of the brain) (`FACT CHECK`) can result in preserving the flow state. Can it prevent the scenario described above. Can this ultimately result in more creative ideas being realized. In my experience, with Cursor and coding, this is the main benefit. It is a bit less dramatic with coding because of the ability to represent an idea before it is built. We have the ability to save our initial idea, to spec it out, to write down the exact requirements. In music, this intermediate step isn't well defined. We could use natural language to write an "outline" of what we want a track to sound like, but attempt to do this and you will find that it does not translate as cleanly. Imagine you are struck with inspiration during lunchtime and instead of hopping into the DAW you attempt to capture this:

> The idea is a techno track at 140bpm. The main element is an industrial-sounding synth drone. The drone is *pulsing* and *moving around*. The hi-hats are *driving*, on every 16th note, with a reverb making them sound like they are *in a factory*. The kick is long, like a repetitive *sonic boom*. There is a vocal that says "do it" *over and over*. It is a female vocal that is notieably chopped from some other sample, maybe an old funk or soul track. The arrangement consists mostly of bringing in and out these elements to create tension and release. There should be an energetic peak, a more low-energy period in the middle, and then a final, climatic section with all elements together.

^ As I was writing this I realized what this looks like. It's a generative music prompt waiting to happen. So I got inspired to try and throw it into Suno, in the name of science. Here is what it gave. Note that this was the free version, so the model is not as capable, it's the "mini", but in order to a) avoid giving them my money so I can keep it for the hopes of actually paying an artist who otherwise would get no compensation for their part in the creation of this clip and b) illustrate the point, I think it's enough.
[https://suno.com/s/dFn126f6gNCN3ozF](https://suno.com/s/dFn126f6gNCN3ozF)

This essay isn't meant to be a critique on Suno or services like it. But my point here can simply just be: listen to how many liberties the model took with my initial prompt. Nowhere in my prompt do I describe a string melody, but you get something that sounds a bit like a Beethoven symphony being mercilessly stamped out on an assembly line at Elon's Tesla factory. You have been warned, I am not an unbiased source when it comes to techno... :) I don't think I need to or should say any more here.

But let's think about if Suno didn't exist and all we had was that description. In coding, you would very quickly get to a point where the level of detail described is not enough to accomplish the task. That, or, if we assume that there is to be nothing else that is not mentioned in the description, we get a very simple track, which is more what I was going for. Either way, my point is there will inevitably be details, design decisions, that are surfaced when going to implement. In that description, there is no mention of a bass, any drums besides hi hats, what frequencies does the drone synth mainly occupy, is it tinny, is it bright, is it dark, is it distorted, etc.
As someone who has worked on the product management side of engineering as well, what you don't want is for the implementation to invent decisions you don't know about. Because if that happens, the product starts to deviate from what you intended, because the details add up to the whole experience. That is exactly what happens with Suno and how you get the sugar-coated LED hellscape in the clip (here again is my bias. If you like the track no judgement. The point is, it isn't what I wanted.)

What you do you want is full control over the design details without needing to know every single implementation detail. That is the abstraction offered by LLM assistants. You have the ability to say "that's not right, it needs to be more 'x'" and the model can choose a different implementation that produces the desired output and also works with the rest of the system. This is the motivation for Chatrack. You can be a creator with full autonomy without being an operator. In a perfect world (to me), this is what generative offerings like Suno would do. But there is so much nuance in sound and perception that is difficult to capture and I do not think we are anywhere close. Never mind the question of "why would we want that" which is for another day. 

Chatrack is intentionally limited to mixing for this reason. The difference between creating a full song and creating a mix is that the implementation details completely map onto a set of controls. You can assume that the desired goal can be achieve solely by adding and adjusting parameters on already-existing and deterministic devices and then passing the existing signal(s) through. These parameters have the double benefit of being represented in code, which is ultimately a textual representation that an LLM can reason over. This brings us to where the project is at currently and the limitations.

Currently chatrack operates on one audio signal only. Multitrack is theoretically possible and planned but requires architecture shifts that will be a lift, namely the need of a structure to hold multiple tracks. This is where you start crossing the line from one plugin to a full DAW. That's a line we're not crossing at the time of writing.
Importantly, **Chatrack is not attempting to make its own mix decisions**. This is a crucial detail. No decision-making is offloaded to the model. The human must still be the one driving. What the model is there to do is to remove the friction between the human knowing what they want and knowing which tools and knobs to reach for to make it so. This keeps the human in control of design details while leaving the implementation details to the model. This is the sweet spot of abstraction that we want. The advantage to this split is that design details are ultimately what the creative process is all about, if you take the assumption that we are working under "creativity as engineering" the first of the creativity frameworks described earlier. Not everyone works that way. If tweaking knobs with no particular outcome in mind is an important part of your creative process, Chatrack probably isn't for you. This is part of why the scope is limited to mixing - it is much more likely to converge on some optimal mix or some rough range where things sound "good". The problem with applying this to full production is that the definition of "good" completely breaks down. In a mix there is some accepted objectivity in the definition of "good". Can you hear the important elements clearly. Does it allow the ideas that are trying to be communciated through the music to come through. The point here is not what makes a good mix, but that the solution space is much more manageable. If you open it up to "what makes a good song" that is effectively entirely subjective (although if you ask Suno, it would say "whatever was in my training set")


## Who is Chatrack for?


You have to know what you want. Chatrack is not going to spit out a perfect mixdown for you. There are plenty of tools and research towards doing that. You can go throw your stems in iZotope Neutron / Ozone which have been around forever (were actually the products that inspired me to work on plugins myself). No LLM needed.

*The thesis for Chatrack is the control paradigm*, not "can AI mix my tune". It's "do we prefer chatting to turning knobs manually". This is so important to state clearly and I have had my own experience of having to make this decision recently while the project was already well underway and the PoC was essentially there.

The key thing that you are handing off to Chatrack is mouse clicks and the internal map of where devices live and how you interact with them. The key hypothesis is that not needing to "go find" the EQ saves you a few precious seconds and more importantly keeps your brain in the abstract, in the creative, not the operational. (Mix and mastering engineers will tell you that mixing and mastering are creative acts. The canvas is the listener's focus, attention, and perception. It is more of a careful curation and scultping of existing material for a certain experience and feeling.)

#### Who does this serve?

Think of three engineers: 

**Alice** is a professional mix engineer. She has a lot of third-party tools, some favorite ones she prefers in different circumstances (for example: the Elysia museq high end band sounds really nice on cymbals, I might try that here). Surfacing a stock EQ may or may not help her. Part of her workflow is based on years of experience with certain tools that she prefers. This is also her signature taste. If Chatrack can work with third-party devices, maybe she is interested. We are not there yet. She knows the design details, can communicate them, but has a non-trivial investment in the implementation details as well. Chatrack is not for Alice (yet?).

**Bob** is a beginner engineer. He mainly is a producer who knows what he wants to make and he knows how professional mixes sound and that there is a gap between the things he makes and what he hears on the radio. He doesn't know what a concept like "warmth" means and he cannot communicate what he wants via language because he does not understand the underlying science behind his stuff and the pro stuff. Explicitly, in its current form, Chatrack is not for him either. The assumption that the user can handle autonomy over the design details is broken here.

**Charlie** is a longtime hobbyist producer who has mixed many of his own and a few other people's records. He can speak mixing language and understands concepts like "warmer, brighter, tinny" and what sound they are referring to. He works a full-time job and doesn't have a lot of time to make music, so he would prefer to be as efficient as possible while still getting his ideas to translate. He doesn't spend enough time in the DAW to remember exactly what tools he has or where to click to bring them up. He hasn't built the deep knowledge of the nuance in sound between different analog EQ's like Alice has, but he can hear the difference between a 6 dB bump at 2k and 10k. In it's current state, Chatrack is best suited for Charlie. But it's still not really "for" him. (Charlie is me, surprise.) The bet would be that maybe more Charlies exist who might benefit from this exact division of cognitive load.

Right now the main question is does Charlie want to still touch some knobs manually. Or would he rather describe to the model what is off: "too much", "a bit more", or "oops too warm". This sort of redefines the Charlies we are looking for as a user base. Those are the kinds of things I'm currently working through, and that's where the project sits currently. To this point, I haven't reached for Chatrack on any of my actual projects yet, and it will probably be a while before I do...

But to be honest, right now, Chatrack isn't "for" anyone. It's simply an experiment, spawned by my own personal curiosity and overlap in interests of technology and music. This may be the one time where I am employing "creativity as play" instead of knowing exactly what I want from the get go. And there is something really fresh and exciting to me about spending more time just exploring and tinkering. There is less pressure on it almost. 

Future goals:
Multitrack

Experimenting on my own tracks whether I want to say "more brighter" or reach for the knob itself. Do I like having the knob I need surfaced immediately?

User-specific context in memory. Should the system start to remember mixing preferences? (see open questions)

Expanding control to third-party devices. Mozart AI recently shipped something like this for their own browser-based DAW (similar to Suno Studio) - [Mozart Scout](https://mozartai.com/scout?mode=vibe). This might open it up to the Alices.

Some open questions:

How much knowledge of the actual audio does the model need?

Should the model live on your computer or is pinging OpenAI over a network enough? How much does latency affect the flow state, and does the tradeoff of not having to dig for the tool offset this? 

Would you uninstall it the day it is slower than grabbing Pro-Q, or the day the knob feels like fighting the product?

Does learning from what you keep versus undo amplify your ear or replace it? (The memory question)

Should people pay for this? Should money be routed back to the people who generating the training data?

And the big one:
Should this exist? Would it help people? Would it augment human creativity or replace it?

If I feel like I have some attempts at answering these questions, I'll let you know. For now all we can do is keep creating.
